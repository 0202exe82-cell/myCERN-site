What is Antimatter?

Antimatter is the opposite of ordinary matter. Every particle has a corresponding antiparticle with the same mass but opposite electric charge. For example, the antiparticle of the electron is the positron, and the antiparticle of the proton is the antiproton.

When matter and antimatter meet, they annihilate — they disappear and release a huge amount of pure energy according to Einstein's equation E = mc².

How is Antimatter Made?

At CERN, antimatter is produced in particle accelerators. The process is extremely complex and inefficient:

  • Protons are accelerated and smashed into a metal target.
  • The collisions create a spray of new particles, including antiprotons.
  • Antiprotons are captured and slowed down in a special decelerator called the Antiproton Decelerator (AD).
  • They are then combined with positrons to form antihydrogen atoms.

CERN is the only place in the world where antihydrogen atoms can be trapped and studied for long periods.

Why is it so difficult?

  • Antimatter annihilates instantly on contact with normal matter.
  • It must be stored in a perfect vacuum using magnetic traps.
  • Producing just 1 gram of antimatter would take billions of years and cost more than the entire world economy.

Despite these challenges, studying antimatter may help answer one of the biggest mysteries of physics: why the universe is made almost entirely of matter, and not equal parts matter and antimatter.
