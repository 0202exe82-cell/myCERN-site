History of CERN

From post-war cooperation to the discovery of the Higgs boson

How it all began

CERN was founded in the aftermath of World War II, when a group of visionary scientists and politicians wanted to rebuild European science and promote peaceful cooperation between nations. The name CERN comes from the French "Conseil Européen pour la Recherche Nucléaire" (European Council for Nuclear Research), established in 1952.

Two years later, in 1954, the organization was officially renamed and its first laboratory was built near Geneva.

Key Milestones

1954 CERN is officially founded by 12 European countries.
1957 The Synchrocyclotron, CERN's first accelerator, begins operation.
1983 Carlo Rubbia and Simon van der Meer discover the W and Z bosons — a major step toward the Standard Model.
1989 Tim Berners-Lee invents the World Wide Web at CERN to help scientists share information.
2008 The Large Hadron Collider (LHC) is switched on — the largest machine ever built by humans.
2012 The Higgs boson is discovered at CERN, confirming a 50-year-old theory and completing the Standard Model of particle physics.
Today CERN continues to push the boundaries of science, studying antimatter, dark matter, and the fundamental laws of the universe.

Why History Matters

CERN is a unique example of international collaboration. Today it has 23 member states and welcomes scientists from over 100 countries. Its history shows how science can unite people across borders, cultures, and generations.
